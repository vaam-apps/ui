# Surfaces and registers

Most design systems have one answer to "how does a box read as a box":
a border, or a shadow, picked once. This library has three, and the
distinction between them is not decorative — it is **what the reader is
doing on that surface**.

- **Diagnostic** — tables, drawers, detail panes. You *read* these, row
  by row, looking for the one that is wrong. A 1px hairline on a surface
  step, and nothing else competing. This is most of the library.
- **Floating** — popover, dropdown, dialog, tooltip, drawer, toast.
  These overlap a ground they do not know, so they get a shadow — M3's
  elevation (level 2 for menus and popovers, level 3 for dialogs and
  toasts) plus an `outline-variant` hairline, because a black shadow on a
  near-black page is invisible.
- **Instrument** — dashboards and metrics. You *scan* these. The job is
  to make one number findable in a glance. These get chrome no other
  register wears: `InstrumentPanel`'s Expressive shapes, `Card glow`'s
  halo, and `Skeleton instrument`'s drift — all three in one hue family,
  the seed's `primary-container`.

`theme.css` states the default directly: borders, not shadows, for
cards, tables and panels; only floating layers get one. The instrument
register is a deliberate exception to that rule and not a loosening of
it — the rule still governs every surface where it was doing work.

## Choosing between them

The question to ask is not "how important is this box" but "what is the
reader's motion across it".

**Reach for diagnostic** when the surface holds more than one comparable
thing and the reader is working through them. Forty table rows, a detail
list, a payload inspector. A hairline is the cheapest boundary that does
not compete with the content, and forty of them in a column read as one
grid rather than forty objects.

**Reach for floating** only when the surface genuinely overlaps content
it cannot know about. A shadow is how a layer says "I am in front", and
a border cannot do that job for something that sits *over* rather than
*beside*. The rule has one deliberate exception: `SideNav`'s floating
rail, which is M3's floating toolbar and carries **no** shadow, because
androidx ships it at elevation `Level0` and the library follows M3
exactly there. It is permanent chrome floating unanchored over
unknown-contrast content — the case the rule exists for — so its fill is
doing a job a shadow would share: a standard bar is `surface-2`, the
same fill as `Card` and `StatTile` in both themes, and over them it loses
its edge. `toolbarVariant="vibrant"` is the answer for a screen where that
happens.

**Reach for instrument** when the surface is a single readable
instrument — a row of metrics, a chart, a headline figure and its
denominator — and repetition is currently doing the grouping. The
component that named this register exists because `StatTile`'s own doc
had already described it: *"tiles appear in rows of three to six, and six
shadowed boxes is visual noise where six outlined ones read as one
instrument panel."* That sentence names a surface the library did not
have. Tiles were getting the effect by repetition — six identical
outlines the eye groups — which works right up until something else on
the screen is also outlined.

`InstrumentPanel` is an M3 Expressive container: `surface-container`, the
extra-large (28px) corner, 24px of padding, and **no border** and no
shadow. The hairline is what makes a `Card` an outlined card and the
diagnostic register's mark — a panel with one would be a big card — and a
shadow would say it floats. What tells it apart instead is the corner, a
4-leaf clover over a quieter 9-sided cookie (two of M3 Expressive's shapes)
tucked into its top-right corner, and what happens to the tiles inside: a
`StatTile` in a panel drops its hairline for a filled
`surface-container-highest` with a 20px corner, and an `emphasized` one
grows its figure to display-small, the Expressive hero size.

`Card glow` is the same register at card scale, and it is the same
discipline: a glow on every card is a glow on none.

## The chrome carries no meaning

This is the load-bearing constraint of the whole register, and without it
the register could not exist here at all.

The library's premise is that **colour means status**. A coloured surface
in such a system is a contradiction waiting to be read as one — unless
the colour is provably incapable of carrying a state. So:

- `InstrumentPanel`'s shapes are the seed's own `primary-container`,
  mixed into the container — the one hue family this system reserves for
  chrome. Not `secondary` or `tertiary`: Expressive rotates those toward
  the status greens at this seed. And a clover, not a small whole cookie,
  because a scalloped disc beside a figure reads as a "verified" seal.
- `Card glow`'s halo (`--instrument-glow`) and `Skeleton instrument`'s
  drift (`--skeleton-instrument-*`) are mixed from the same family, so
  the register has one palette rather than one per component.
- There is **no per-state variant** and **no way for a caller to tint
  it**. `InstrumentPanel` takes a title, a caption and children. Nothing
  else.
- Nothing infers a state from a shape or a glow, and no caller can
  express one through either.

The hue vocabulary stays entirely with `StatusHue`. That is the only
reason a coloured surface is safe here, and it is why none of these
paints takes a colour from a caller.

## The aurora ramp, deprecated

`Card glow` and `Skeleton instrument` used to paint with the `--aurora-*`
ramp — four stops bound by `color-mix` to the status hues, an exception
to "colour means status" that every comment about it had to argue for.
The panel moved off it first (its mesh became the Expressive shapes);
the glow and the skeleton followed, so the register no longer has two
colour schemes. `--aurora-*`, `--skeleton-aurora-*`, `.aurora-glow` and
`.aurora-mesh` are still in `theme.css`, unchanged, for a consumer who
applied them by hand; nothing in the package renders them, and they go
in the next major.

The history is kept because it is the reasoning the light-theme values
below still follow. The ramp was once less honest than its comment. The first version
declared it "named rather than borrowed" while all four values were in
fact the status palette copy-pasted as literals, with nothing binding
them and the two themes borrowing inconsistently. They are `color-mix`
derived now and the comment says what is true. The arc was also too
narrow to read as several hues — `--aurora-3` sat on `--ring`, between
the cyan and the violet it was meant to separate, so the mesh read as one
hue with a gradient. It is a mix toward `--state-danger-fg` now, a rose
between `parked`'s fuchsia and `danger`, with the stop alphas raised
(dark 30/26/22/14, light 20/17/15/10, from 22/18/16/10 and 14/12/11/7).
It was kept off the seed's `primary` on the argument that the primary hue
is the interactive one and a glow is not a control. That argument lost to
a simpler one: two colour schemes in one register read as two registers,
and the primary family is the only hue this system can paint chrome in
without arguing for an exception.

## `--subtle-foreground` is banned on the panel

A shaped ground breaks the assumption every contrast check in this
system rests on: that a foreground sits on **one** known surface. The
panel keeps the damage countable. Its shapes are opaque mixes of
`primary-container` into the container — `--instrument-shape` for the
clover, `--instrument-shape-quiet` for the halo — never transparency, so
there are exactly three grounds, and `contrast.test.ts` resolves the mixes
and measures every tier on each. The worst of the three is the clover:

So `InstrumentPanel` renders its own caption at `muted` and says so in
its module doc, rather than documenting a rule and trusting every call
site to follow it. `StatusPill` makes the identical move for the
identical reason when a loud tint lands under its label. **Do not put
`text-subtle-foreground` on this surface** — it is the one tier the
clover can swallow. The gate asserts it still fails there, so the ban is
lifted the day it stops being needed rather than outliving its reason.

The shares are measured ceilings, not taste: `--muted-foreground` stays
above 4.5:1 on the clover up to a 23% share of `primary-container` in
dark and 46% in light; the panel ships 12% and 36%. The old aurora mesh
had the same ban for the same reason — subtle at 4.15:1 and 4.25:1 at the
blobs' peak alpha.

Switch the theme in the toolbar while looking at that story. It is
marginal in both, and on the bare container subtle even passes, which is
exactly the kind of "looks fine" that ships a failure the moment a caption
runs into the corner. The capped share and the tier swap are the two
halves of one decision; neither survives alone.

The panel is also **static**, and not interactive — no state layer,
nothing for reduced motion to stop. A drifting ground behind a number you are
trying to read is a different thing from one standing in for a number
that has not arrived — that is `Skeleton`'s job, and its own drift is
transform-only on coprime periods for reasons of its own.

## The light-theme glow, and the fix that made it worse

This one is worth a section because the principled-looking move was
measured and rejected, and the finding is written into `theme.css` so
nobody re-derives it. It was found on the deprecated aurora glow; it is
why the light `--instrument-glow` and `--skeleton-instrument-*` lean on
`primary` rather than on `primary-container`.

The aurora glow painted four offset shadows in four hues. In dark they
read as four. **In light they collapsed into one grey-lilac smudge.**

The cause is a constraint imported from the wrong place. Light's
`--state-*-fg` values are dark and desaturated *because they are text
colours* — chosen to clear 4.5:1 on a near-white page. A glow carries no
text. The ramp had inherited a type constraint that does not apply to it.

The obvious fix is to mix each stop toward white, freeing it from that
constraint. Rendered, that is **strictly worse**: a tint mixed toward
white sits *closer* to a white ground, so every stop lost the contrast
that made it visible at all, and the aurora went from green-and-lilac to
flat grey. On a light ground, saturation against the ground is the entire
budget.

What actually works is alpha. Light keeps the darker, more chromatic
values and spends its budget on opacity instead: a `[data-theme="light"]`
override takes the four glow stops to **78/72/66/58%**, against dark's
60/55/50/40%. The aurora skeleton went the other way — light's stops were
*lower* than dark's, because the same values do more work on a near-white ground
and at dark's strengths the light skeleton reads as periwinkle rather
than as a tint.

The same rule, applied to one hue: light `primary-container` (#bdbaff) on
a near-white page is a tint, so the light halo is a `primary-container`
ring for the colour plus two `primary` shadows (30% and 38%) for the
depth, where dark gets away with `primary-container` at 18% and 40% and
one `primary` shadow at 36%. The light skeleton spends 72% and 56% of
`primary-container` on its strong tiers and 22% and 14% of `primary` on
its faint ones; dark keeps the tonal falloff, 30/26/22/14%.

Text is never placed on the halo inside the library, but a caller's caption
can sit under a glowing card, so the halo was measured there — the worst
pixel of a row below the card, in Chromium. `--muted-foreground` clears
4.5:1 from 4px below in dark (4.78:1) and from 8px in light (4.55:1);
`--subtle-foreground` only from 16px (4.75:1 and 4.67:1). The aurora glow
was worse at every distance: subtle at 3.69:1 and 3.30:1 even 16px away.
Keep such a caption `muted`, and at least a `gap-2` from the card.

One consequence of the old block is worth knowing. Its light override was
`[data-theme="light"] .aurora-glow`, specificity (0,2,0), so the
reduced-motion softening had to be spelled out for **both** selectors:
a bare `.aurora-glow` inside the media query is (0,1,0), and specificity
beats source order. `.instrument-glow` avoids the trap by construction:
the whole shadow list is a per-theme token, `--instrument-glow` (and
`--instrument-glow-reduced`), so the rule and its reduced-motion form are
one selector each and there is no theme selector to lose to.

## Two implementation notes that look like bugs

**The glow is stacked `box-shadow`s, not a blurred pseudo-element.** The
pseudo-element version is the obvious one and it does not survive contact
with a real page: it needs `z-index: -1` to sit behind the card's own
opaque background, and a negative-z-index element paints in the *root*
stacking context's negative layer, which is below in-flow block
backgrounds. Any page whose `body` has a background colour paints
straight over it — this library's own Storybook does exactly that, and so
does essentially every consuming application. Every computed style
correct, every value present, and no glow on screen. `box-shadow` paints
outside the border box in the element's own painting order, where no
ancestor background can get in front of it.

**Neither register uses `filter: blur()`.** The skeleton bans it on a
cost argument that `theme.css` records — a blurred layer is cheap once
and ruinous at two hundred table cells, where it would promote every cell
to its own composited layer — and the argument was expected to invert for
a dashboard's six cards. It never got the chance to: the shipped glow is
`box-shadow`, for the stacking-context reason above, and that costs no
filter, no extra layer and no stacking assumptions. Nothing in the
stylesheet declares a blur today.
